## 1. Key Terms

| Term | Short definition (handout wording, simplified) | Memory hook |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Level Design** | The creation of environments, scenarios, or missions in an electronic game, centered on a unifying theme/function. | Building the stage, mission, and mood around one core idea. |
| **Structure** | Using levels to divide a game into effective subdivisions, organize progression, and enhance gameplay (via objectives, flow, duration, availability, relationships, progression). | The skeleton of levels holding the game together. |
| **Objectives** | A set of goals the player understands for each level, taught via briefings (cut-scenes/tutorials), status screens, or immediate action. | What the player knows they need to do to beat the level. |
| **Flow** | Controlling player movement so they stay in an area until objectives are met and cannot return once finished. | One-way traffic through a level's areas. |
| **Natural Barriers** | Obstacles in open-world levels that are destroyed as a by-product of the player's progress, keeping them from running past enemies. | Barriers that break only when you actually progress. |
| **One-Way Barrier** | Closing off a completed area (e.g., a door locking behind the player) to prevent backtracking and signal progress. | Lock-behind-you door = forward progress. |
| **Duration** | How much time should be spent on each level; universally, a player should finish at least one level in a single session. | Level length matching the player's session. |
| **Milestones of Achievement** | Regular markers of advancement or task completion required in games with much longer sessions. | Checkpoints/wins that keep long sessions rewarding. |
| **Availability** | How many levels are open to the player at once (one, a small number, or many), matched to gameplay goals and genre. | How many doors are unlocked at the same time. |
| **Relationship** | How levels connect to one another—either through increasing difficulty (puzzle games) or storylines (episodic). | How level 1 connects to level 2. |
| **Episodic Relationship** | A storyline-based relationship where each level is self-contained with its own internal plotline and conclusion (like TV). | Every level is its own TV episode. |
| **Progression** | Pacing the game through level design by slowly increasing difficulty, building conflict in arcs, and varying the pace. | Slow ramp-up + alternating action, exploration, and puzzles. |
| **Time** | How "game time" relates to real-world time (slower, faster, equal, or nonexistent until the player interacts). | The clock inside the game vs. the clock on your wall. |
| **Authentic Time** | Portraying time authentically and using the passage of real-world time as a gameplay mechanic (e.g., *Boktai*'s light sensor). | Real-world time/light directly drives the gameplay. |
| **Limited Time** | Time used as setting/atmosphere without altering gameplay, OR giving limited real-world time under pressure (e.g., *FarmVille* crops). | Either cosmetic day/night OR a real-world countdown to act. |
| **Player-Adjusted Time** | Letting players modify the length of time associated with game levels (e.g., 5–10 min vs. 15 min football quarters). | Player picks the match/quarter length. |
| **Altered Time** | Using manipulated time as a gameplay effect, such as slow motion or stopping time (e.g., *Max Payne*'s bullet time). | Slow-mo or freeze-time superpowers. |
| **Bullet Time** | Going into slow motion while retaining the ability to move the camera's viewpoint at normal speed (*Max Payne* / *The Matrix*). | Slow-mo action + normal-speed camera pan. |
| **Space** | The physical environment of the game, including perspective, scale, boundaries, structures, terrain, objects, and style. | The entire physical world the player moves through. |
| **Camera Systems** | Systems in video games used to show action at the best angle, especially in 3D worlds needing a third-person POV. | The virtual cameraman framing 3D action. |
| **Point-of-View (POV)** | How the player views the game environment; directly tied to the perspective of the game world. | The eyes/angle through which you see the world. |
| **Omnipresent Perspective** | Looking down from above with the ability to view and act in many different locations of the game world (even if parts are hidden). | God-view: see and act anywhere across the map. |
| **Aerial (Top-Down) Perspective** | Showing the game strictly as seen from directly above—a bird's-eye view. | Straight-down bird's-eye camera. |
| **Isometric Perspective** | Looking slightly across the landscape at a 30- to 45-degree angle; uses reusable pre-angled objects instead of real-time rendering. | Angled 30°–45° view that feels closer than top-down. |
| **Side-Scrolling (Flat/Side View)** | 2D navigation where characters only move left/right or jump up/down while the background scrolls horizontally. | Classic 2D arcade side view. |
| **Parallax Scrolling** | Moving camera vertically or horizontally with different background layers moving at different speeds to create a feeling of depth. | Far layers move slow, near layers move fast = 2D depth. |
| **Environmental Materials** | Substances like metal, glass, sand, gravel, sky, and clouds that directly influence the look and feel of the game. | What things in the world are made of. |
| **Shading Model** | Defines how materials behave when lit by combining material attributes (texture, color, shininess, translucency) with light attributes (color, direction). | Material traits + light traits = how surfaces look lit. |
| **Terrain** | Textures that appear on ground surfaces, such as dirt, grass, tile, and pavement. | The skin/texture on the ground. |
| **Radiosity (Lighting)** | Lighting effects that allow players to navigate, see/interact with progression details, and see reflections on water or glass. | Lighting that makes details readable and surfaces reflective. |
| **Environmental Game Effects** | Visual/animated effects beyond lighting, including climate (rain, snow, lightning) and natural movements (waves, wind, flotation). | Weather and natural motion in the world. |
| **Scale** | The total size of the physical space and the relative sizes of objects within the game (critical in simulation games). | How big the world and objects are compared to each other. |
| **Realism** | Modeling the real world (e.g., photographic and land-height data in flight simulators); adding detail trades off speed and efficiency. | Real-world data & physics—more detail = slower performance. |
| **Style** | The visual/thematic identity of the game world (influencing character, interface, manual, packaging), combining object style and artwork style. | Object style + artwork style working together for an emotion. |

## 2. Look-Alike / Confusing Terms

### Group A: Structure Pillars (Flow vs. Progression vs. Relationship vs. Availability)
| Term | What it is | Key difference | Clue words in a question |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Flow** | Controlling movement within a level's areas | Uses **barriers** (natural or one-way) to keep players in or out of areas | "stay in an area", "run past opponents", "prevent returning", "door locks behind" |
| **Progression** | Pacing difficulty and conflict across/within levels | Focuses on **difficulty curve**, **conflict arcs**, and **varying pace** | "slowly increases difficulty", "series of arcs", "vary the pace", "only experts survive" |
| **Relationship** | How levels connect to each other | Focuses on **storyline (episodic)** or **difficulty link** between levels | "scene or episode", "self-contained", "internal plotline", "puzzle games" |
| **Availability** | How many levels are open at one time | Matches **number of open levels** (1, small, many) to **genre** | "one level at a time", "first-person immersion", "RPG quests", "RTS/process sims" |

> **Trap Warning:** Both *Progression* and *Relationship* mention increasing difficulty, but *Relationship* asks how levels connect (e.g., puzzle levels linked only by difficulty vs. episodic story), while *Progression* is about pacing, conflict arcs, and avoiding unfair expert-only spikes.

### Group B: Time Types (Authentic vs. Limited vs. Player-Adjusted vs. Altered)
| Term | What it is | Key difference | Clue words in a question |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Authentic** | Real-world time/environment drives gameplay | Uses **real time/sensors** as a core mechanic (*Boktai*) | "light sensor", "played outside during the day", "correct time of day" |
| **Limited** | Time as setting only, OR real-world countdown pressure | Either **doesn't alter gameplay** (cosmetic day/night) OR **real-world deadline** (*FarmVille*) | "part of the setting", "feel artificial", "limited real-world time", "wilted crops" |
| **Player-Adjusted** | Player chooses level/match time length | Player **modifies duration** (e.g., 5–10 min vs. 15 min quarters) | "modify the time", "shorter quarters", "sports games", "time options" |
| **Altered** | Time manipulated as a special effect | **Slow motion** or **stopping time** (*Max Payne* bullet time) | "slow motion", "bullet time", "camera at normal speed", "stop time" |

> **Trap Warning:** Don't confuse *Authentic* with *Limited*! *Authentic* integrates real-world time/conditions into how the game is played (*Boktai*), whereas *Limited* is either purely cosmetic setting or a real-world waiting/pressure window (*FarmVille*).

### Group C: Perspectives (Omnipresent vs. Aerial/Top-Down vs. Isometric vs. Side-Scrolling)
| Term | What it is | Key difference | Clue words in a question |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Omnipresent** | View and act across many parts of the world from above | Emphasizes **acting in many locations** even if parts are hidden | "different parts of the game world", "take actions in many locations", "parts are hidden" |
| **Aerial (Top-Down)** | Straight-down view from above | Pure **bird's-eye view** with no angled tilt | "seen from above", "bird's-eye view" |
| **Isometric** | Angled 30°–45° view across the landscape | Uses **reusable pre-angled objects**; feels closer than top-down | "30- to 45-degree angle", "reusable objects", "feel closer and more involved" |
| **Side-Scrolling** | 2D flat/side view with horizontal movement | Characters only move **left/right or up/down**; uses **parallax scrolling** | "2D space", "left to right", "jump up and down", "parallax scrolling" |

> **Trap Warning:** *Omnipresent* and *Aerial (Top-Down)* both look down from above, but *Aerial* strictly describes the bird's-eye camera angle, while *Omnipresent* emphasizes the player's ability to view and act across multiple locations of the world.

### Group D: Surface & Visual Elements (Terrain vs. Materials vs. Shading Model vs. Radiosity vs. Style)
| Term | What it is | Key difference | Clue words in a question |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Terrain** | Textures on **ground surfaces** only | Dirt, grass, tile, pavement | "ground surfaces", "dirt, grass, tile, pavement" |
| **Environmental Materials** | Substances making up world elements | Metal, glass, sand, gravel, sky, clouds | "metal, glass, sand, gravel, sky, clouds" |
| **Shading Model** | Rule for how materials look when lit | Combines **material attributes** + **light attributes** | "behave when they are lit", "shininess and translucency", "light color and direction" |
| **Radiosity** | Lighting and reflection effects | Enables **navigation, detail interaction, and reflections** | "navigate", "see and interact with details", "reflection on water, glass" |
| **Style** | Artistic & object identity of the game world | Combines **object style** + **artwork style** for emotion | "Spanish-style homes", "anime", "character, interface, manual, packaging" |

> **Trap Warning:** *Terrain* is strictly ground-surface textures, whereas *Environmental Materials* includes broader world elements like sky, clouds, metal, and glass; *Shading Model* is the formula combining those materials with light.

## 3. Likely Distractors

- **Level Design**
  - *Distractors:* Structure, Space, Style.
  - *How to tell apart:* Level Design is the overall creation of environments/scenarios/missions around a unifying theme; Structure organizes progression/flow, Space is the physical world, and Style is the visual/artistic look.
- **Natural Barriers**
  - *Distractors:* One-Way Barrier, Flow, Availability.
  - *How to tell apart:* Natural Barriers stop players from skipping forward (destroyed by progress); One-Way Barriers stop players from going backward (lock behind you).
- **One-Way Barrier**
  - *Distractors:* Natural Barriers, Flow, Episodic Relationship.
  - *How to tell apart:* One-Way Barriers lock completed areas behind the player; Natural Barriers block forward skipping until objectives are met.
- **Duration**
  - *Distractors:* Player-Adjusted Time, Limited Time, Progression.
  - *How to tell apart:* Duration is the designer's target length per level (e.g., 15 min, 45 min, 2 hrs); Player-Adjusted Time lets the player change match/quarter length; Limited Time is setting or real-world deadline pressure.
- **Availability**
  - *Distractors:* Flow, Relationship, Progression.
  - *How to tell apart:* Availability is how many levels are unlocked at once (1, small number, many); Flow is movement inside a level; Relationship is how levels connect.
- **Episodic Relationship**
  - *Distractors:* Progression, Availability, Objectives.
  - *How to tell apart:* Episodic Relationship means each level is a self-contained story with its own plotline and conclusion (like TV), whereas Progression is pacing/difficulty.
- **Progression**
  - *Distractors:* Relationship, Flow, Duration.
  - *How to tell apart:* Progression is pacing difficulty and conflict arcs; Relationship is how levels link together; Flow is area entry/exit control.
- **Authentic Time**
  - *Distractors:* Limited Time, Realism, Altered Time.
  - *How to tell apart:* Authentic Time uses real-world time/sensors as a gameplay mechanic (*Boktai*); Limited Time is setting-only or real-world waiting (*FarmVille*); Altered Time slows/stops time (*Max Payne*).
- **Limited Time**
  - *Distractors:* Authentic Time, Player-Adjusted Time, Duration.
  - *How to tell apart:* Limited Time either doesn't affect gameplay (cosmetic day/night) or pressures players with a real-world time window (*FarmVille*).
- **Player-Adjusted Time**
  - *Distractors:* Duration, Limited Time, Altered Time.
  - *How to tell apart:* Player-Adjusted Time lets players pick shorter/longer level timers (e.g., football quarters), unlike Altered Time (slow-mo/freeze in-game) or Duration (designer's level length).
- **Altered Time (Bullet Time)**
  - *Distractors:* Authentic Time, Player-Adjusted Time, Parallax Scrolling.
  - *How to tell apart:* Altered Time slows or stops in-game time as a mechanic/effect (e.g., normal camera speed during slow motion).
- **Omnipresent Perspective**
  - *Distractors:* Aerial (Top-Down), Isometric, Camera Systems.
  - *How to tell apart:* Omnipresent lets you view and act in many locations from above (even with hidden parts); Aerial is just the bird's-eye camera angle.
- **Aerial (Top-Down) Perspective**
  - *Distractors:* Omnipresent, Isometric, Side-Scrolling.
  - *How to tell apart:* Aerial is straight down (bird's-eye); Isometric is tilted 30°–45°; Omnipresent emphasizes multi-location control.
- **Isometric Perspective**
  - *Distractors:* Aerial (Top-Down), Side-Scrolling, Parallax Scrolling.
  - *How to tell apart:* Isometric uses a 30°–45° angle and reusable pre-angled objects so you don't render in real time.
- **Parallax Scrolling**
  - *Distractors:* Side-Scrolling, Isometric, Radiosity.
  - *How to tell apart:* Parallax Scrolling is specifically the multi-speed layer technique inside 2D Side-Scrolling to create depth.
- **Shading Model**
  - *Distractors:* Radiosity, Terrain, Environmental Materials.
  - *How to tell apart:* Shading Model combines material traits + light traits; Radiosity is the lighting/reflection effect itself; Terrain is ground textures.
- **Radiosity**
  - *Distractors:* Shading Model, Environmental Game Effects, Realism.
  - *How to tell apart:* Radiosity is lighting/reflection needed to navigate and see details; Environmental Game Effects are climate (rain/snow/lightning) and natural motion (waves/wind/flotation).
- **Realism**
  - *Distractors:* Scale, Authentic Time, Style.
  - *How to tell apart:* Realism uses real-world data (photos, land-height) and trades speed/efficiency for detail; Scale is specifically relative/total size; Style is artistic/object aesthetic.

## 4. Lists, Sequences & "Which is NOT"

1. **3 Functions a Level Can Fulfill in Level Design**
   - Introduce a new character or object
   - Focus on a plot point (e.g., discovering a secret or preventing an attack)
   - Create a mood through visuals or a storyline
   - *Fake "NOT" trap:* "Increase hardware rendering speed" or "Replace the game's UI."

2. **6 Considerations When Designing Level Structure**
   - Goal (**Objectives**)
   - **Flow**
   - **Duration**
   - **Availability**
   - **Relationships**
   - Difficulty (**Progression**)
   - *Fake "NOT" trap:* **Radiosity**, **Realism**, or **Style** (those belong to *Space*, not *Structure*).

3. **3 Ways Developers Communicate Level Objectives**
   - Briefing via a cut-scene or interactive tutorial at the start of the level
   - Providing access to a status screen during the course of the game
   - Immediately throwing players into action with easy-to-solve tasks illustrating rules in context
   - *Fake "NOT" trap:* "Providing a printed instruction manual only" or "Locking the player in a one-way barrier."

4. **2 Things to Address for Level Flow**
   - Keep the player in a particular area until necessary objectives are met (**natural barriers** destroyed by progress)
   - Prevent the player from returning to a completed area (**one-way barrier**, e.g., a locking door)
   - *Fake "NOT" trap:* "Letting players choose 5–10 minute quarters" (that's *Player-Adjusted Time*).

5. **3 Duration Benchmarks by Platform / Audience**
   - Computer games (children): **15-minute spurts**
   - Computer games (hardcore gamers): **~2 hours** of continuous concentration
   - Console games: usually **~45 minutes**
   - *(Plus universal rule: complete at least 1 level in a single session; longer sessions need regular **milestones of achievement**.)*
   - *Fake "NOT" trap:* "15-minute quarters" (that's the football example under *Player-Adjusted Time*).

6. **3 Availability Modes & Their Best-Fit Genres**
   - **One level at a time**: Games requiring first-person immersion
   - **Small number of open levels at a time**: RPGs (alleviates frustration when juggling quests)
   - **Many open levels at a time**: Process sims and RTS games (confuses other players)
   - *Fake "NOT" trap:* "Puzzle games" (mentioned under *Relationship*, not *Availability*) or "Flight simulators" (mentioned under *Realism*).

7. **3 Types of Activities to Alternate in Progression Pacing**
   - Struggling to stay alive
   - Systematically exploring the environment
   - Reflectively solving challenging puzzles
   - *Fake "NOT" trap:* "Modifying quarter lengths" or "Designing reusable isometric objects."

8. **4 Types / Aspects of Time**
   - **Authentic** (*Boktai*)
   - **Limited** (*FarmVille* / cosmetic day-night)
   - **Player-Adjusted** (sports game quarters)
   - **Altered** (*Max Payne* bullet time)
   - *Fake "NOT" trap:* **Episodic**, **Omnipresent**, or **Continuous** (not one of the 4 named Time categories).

9. **7 Elements Included in Space**
   - Perspective
   - Scale
   - Boundaries
   - Structures
   - Terrain
   - Objects
   - Style (color, texture, look, and feel)
   - *Fake "NOT" trap:* **Duration** or **Availability** (those belong to *Structure*).

10. **4 Perspectives (plus Parallax Scrolling)**
    - **Omnipresent**
    - **Aerial (Top-Down)**
    - **Isometric** (30°–45°)
    - **Side-Scrolling (Flat/Side View)** (uses **parallax scrolling**)
    - *Fake "NOT" trap:* **First-person POV** (mentioned under Availability/Camera context, not listed as one of the 4 named perspective sub-bullets) or **Radiosity**.

11. **6 Examples of Environmental Materials vs. 4 Examples of Terrain**
    - *Environmental Materials (6):* metal, glass, sand, gravel, sky, clouds
    - *Terrain (4):* dirt, grass, tile, pavement
    - *Fake "NOT" trap:* Swapping "sky" or "clouds" into *Terrain*, or "grass/pavement" into *Environmental Materials*.

12. **2 Attribute Groups Combined by a Shading Model**
    - Material attributes: **texture, color, shininess, translucency**
    - Light source attributes: **color, direction**
    - *Fake "NOT" trap:* "Animation speed" or "Camera angle."

13. **2 Categories of Other Environmental Game Effects (besides Radiosity)**
    - **Climate**: rain, snow, lightning
    - **Natural movements** (via animation): waves, wind, flotation
    - *Fake "NOT" trap:* "Reflection on water/glass" (that's caused by **Radiosity**, not animated natural movements).

14. **2 Main Style Forms That Must Work Together**
    - Style of the **objects** in the world (e.g., Spanish-style homes)
    - Style of the **artwork** depicting the world (e.g., anime)
    - *Fake "NOT" trap:* "Style of the audio" or "Style of the hardware."

15. **4 Emotions Listed to Help Formulate Style**
    - Awe
    - Fear
    - Excitement
    - Amusement
    - *Fake "NOT" trap:* "Frustration" (mentioned under RPG Availability, not Style emotions).

## 6. Last-Minute Cram Sheet

- **Level Design** centers on a unifying theme/function: introduce a character/object, focus on a plot point, or create a mood.
- **6 Structure considerations:** Objectives (goal), Flow, Duration, Availability, Relationships, Progression (difficulty).
- **Flow** = **Natural barriers** (stop skipping forward; destroyed by progress) + **One-way barriers** (stop backtracking; signal progress).
- **Duration** rule: finish at least 1 level per session (~15 min PC kids, ~45 min console, ~2 hrs PC hardcore); longer sessions need **milestones of achievement**.
- **Availability:** 1 level = first-person immersion; small number = RPGs; many open levels = process sims & RTS.
- **Episodic Relationship** = self-contained level with its own internal plotline and conclusion (like TV); puzzle levels link by difficulty.
- **Progression:** slowly increase difficulty, build conflict in arcs, vary pace (survive, explore, solve puzzles), never make it expert-only.
- **4 Time types:** **Authentic** (*Boktai* light sensor), **Limited** (setting-only OR *FarmVille* countdown), **Player-Adjusted** (5–10 min sports quarters), **Altered** (*Max Payne* bullet time = slow-mo + normal camera).
- **4 Perspectives:** **Omnipresent** (act across world from above), **Aerial/Top-Down** (bird's-eye), **Isometric** (30°–45°, reusable objects), **Side-Scrolling** (2D left/right + **parallax scrolling** for depth).
- **Terrain** = ground textures (dirt, grass, tile, pavement); **Environmental materials** = metal, glass, sand, gravel, sky, clouds.
- **Shading model** = material attributes (texture, color, shininess, translucency) + light attributes (color, direction).
- **Radiosity** = lighting for navigation, interacting with details, and reflections (water/glass); **climate** (rain/snow/lightning) and **natural movements** (waves/wind/flotation) are other environmental effects.
- **Realism** uses real photo/height data (flight sims); **more detail = less speed/efficiency**, and all games are abstractions of reality.
- **Style** combines **object style** + **artwork style** consistently, driven by target emotion (**awe, fear, excitement, amusement**).

## Answer Key

1. **B — Natural Barrier.** Natural barriers are destroyed as a by-product of progress to keep players from running past opponents. *A (One-Way Barrier)* is wrong because it prevents returning to a completed area, not skipping ahead.
2. **C — One-Way Barrier.** Closing off a completed area (like a locking door) prevents backtracking and signals progress. *A (Natural Barrier)* is wrong because it blocks forward skipping before objectives are met.
3. **B — Allow a small number of open levels at a time.** The handout states this alleviates frustration for RPG players juggling quests. *A* suits first-person immersion, while *C* suits process sims/RTS and confuses most players.
4. **B — Many open levels fit the gameplay goals of process sims and RTS games.** The handout explicitly notes many open levels confuse most players but work well in process sims and RTS games. *A* and *C* contradict the handout.
5. **B — Episodic relationship.** Each level is self-contained with its own internal plotline and conclusion, like TV. *A* is wrong because puzzle games are linked only by increasing difficulty.
6. **C — Progression.** Progression paces the game via slowly increasing difficulty, conflict arcs, and alternating survival, exploration, and puzzle-solving. *A (Flow)* is wrong because Flow controls area entry/exit with barriers.
7. **A — Duration.** Duration addresses how much time is spent per level (~45 minutes for console games) so at least one level finishes per session. *B (Player-Adjusted Time)* is wrong because it refers to players changing match/quarter lengths.
8. **B — Regular milestones of achievement.** The handout specifies providing milestones (advancement or task completion) regularly for much longer sessions. *A* and *C* are unrelated to session length rewards.
9. **C — Radiosity.** Radiosity is an element of **Space**, not one of the six Structure considerations (Objectives, Flow, Duration, Availability, Relationships, Progression).
10. **B — Authentic Time.** *Boktai* uses a light sensor and real time of day as a gameplay characteristic. *A (Limited Time)* is wrong because Limited Time either doesn't alter gameplay or uses a waiting/countdown window (*FarmVille*).
11. **C — Limited Time.** Both setting-only day/night cycles and real-world time pressure windows (*FarmVille*) fall under Limited Time. *A (Authentic Time)* is wrong because it integrates real-world time/sensors directly into active mechanics (*Boktai*).
12. **D — Player-Adjusted Time.** Letting players choose 5–10 minute quarters instead of 15-minute quarters is Player-Adjusted Time. *A (Altered Time)* is wrong because Altered Time is an in-game slow-mo/stop-time effect.
13. **B — Bullet time.** Bullet time (*Max Payne* / *The Matrix*) slows motion while keeping normal camera viewpoint speed. *A (Parallax scrolling)* is wrong because it is a 2D layered depth technique.
14. **B — Omnipresent.** Omnipresent lets players view and act in many different locations from above, even if parts are hidden. *A (Aerial/Top-Down)* only describes the straight-down bird's-eye angle without the multi-location action emphasis.
15. **A — Isometric.** Isometric uses a 30°–45° angle, reusable objects (avoiding real-time rendering), and feels closer than top-down. *B (Aerial)* is straight down and lacks the 30°–45° angle.
16. **C — Parallax scrolling.** Moving background layers at different speeds when the camera moves gives 2D side-scrollers a feeling of depth. *A (Bullet time)* is an Altered Time slow-mo effect.
17. **C — A shading model.** A shading model combines material attributes (texture, color, shininess, translucency) with light attributes (color, direction). *B (Radiosity)* is the lighting/reflection effect in the environment, not the material-light formula.
18. **B — Radiosity.** Without proper radiosity (lighting), players cannot navigate, interact with progression details, or see reflections on water/glass. *A (Terrain)* only refers to ground textures.
19. **D — Clouds.** Clouds (along with metal, glass, sand, gravel, sky) are listed under **Environmental Materials**, whereas **Terrain** lists dirt, grass, tile, and pavement.
20. **B — Realism.** Realism (like flight simulators using photo/land-height data) notes that adding detail often subtracts speed and efficiency. *A (Scale)* is only about total and relative object sizes.
21. **B — Object style and artwork style.** The handout uses Spanish-style homes (object style) + anime (artwork style) as its exact example of two style forms working together consistently. *A* and *C* are Space rendering/surface elements, not the two Style forms.
22. **C — Level Design.** This is the exact opening definition of Level Design on Page 1. *A (Structure)* is how levels subdivide and organize the game; *B (Space)* is the physical environment.
